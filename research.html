How a gut-bacterial protein makes β-cells grow

My Ph.D. work in the Guillemin Lab asks how microbes shape pancreatic β-cell development, and whether one bacterial protein could help expand or mature β-cells for diabetes therapy. NIH NIDDK F31 fellowship, 2025–2027.

The question

β-cells make insulin. Lose them and you get type 1 diabetes; wear them out and you get type 2.

Yet we still understand little about what makes β-cells grow and mature early in life. Gut microbes turn out to matter: germ-free zebrafish develop fewer β-cells, and a single secreted bacterial protein, BefA (Beta-cell Expansion Factor A), is enough to restore them (Hill et al., 2016; Hill et al., 2022).

My dissertation asks how BefA does it, at the level of the membrane, the cell, and the whole animal.

What I found

BefA acts directly on β-cells.

Combining genetic and pharmacological manipulation, live super-resolution imaging, single-cell RNA-seq, and mitochondrial respirometry, in zebrafish, ex vivo neonatal mouse islets, and cultured mammalian cells, I found that BefA permeabilizes β-cell membranes, drives calcium flux, and promotes mitochondrial function. The result is β-cell proliferation and improved metabolic potential.

Where it lands

β-cell proliferation can be induced with an exogenous protein.

That points to two applications: prophylactically expanding β-cells early in life to help prevent diabetes, and maturing induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived β-cells for transplant.

The work also supported two funded NIH grants for the lab: an R01 on BefA's mechanism and therapeutic potential ($1.29M), and an R61 on engineering mitohormetic probiotics ($1.03M).
